Teacher notes

Goal. Pure morphology: students should be able to build any present subjunctive form. Meaning comes on slide 6, so don't let the class drift into "when do I use it?" yet. Promise them it's coming.

Why start from the yo? The yo form already carries most of the irregularity (tengo, hago, conozco, digo), so the stem comes free. Make it a ritual: say the yo out loud before every form.

The vowel swap. -ar verbs take the -er/-ir vowel and vice versa. One image that sticks is that the verb "changes uniform": same body, new vowel.

Next. The yo trick covers most verbs. Slide 2 deals with the four groups where it needs adjusting.

Bridge. Before moving on, point out that students have already heard these forms in signs and instructions (no fume, pase). Slide 3 makes that explicit.

Teacher notes

Frame it first. Nothing here replaces the yo trick. These are four small adjustments, and three of them are completely predictable. Only the six in section 2 have to be memorized.

Spelling (1). These aren't irregularities; they protect the sound. Have students read *buse or *llege aloud and hear what goes wrong. It only affects -ar verbs, because only they take an e.

The six (2). They're irregular for a neat reason: their yo doesn't end in -o (doy, estoy, he, voy, sé, soy), so the trick has nothing to cut. This makes the list feel logical rather than arbitrary. The tilde on dé separates it from the preposition de; on esté it separates it from este.

-ar / -er stem changers (3). The change follows the yo, but nosotros and vosotros stay regular, exactly as in the present indicative (pensamos → pensemos). The typical error is *piensemos.

-ir stem changers (4). This is the only real surprise on the slide: nosotros and vosotros get a small change (e → i, o → u) even though their present indicative has none (dormimos but durmamos). Point at the highlighted letters in the table and let students find the pattern themselves.

How the exercises escalate. Exercise 1 goes regular → -ir (does it really take a?) → irregular yo (hago, conozco, traigo, veo). Exercise 2 is about the edges: spelling, a double change (empieces: e→ie and z→c), and three of the six.

Teacher notes

Book link. Español en marcha A2, unit 7A ("En vacaciones, sigue estos consejos") teaches only tú and usted, with entrar, beber, salir, poner(se). The rule examples here come from that beach-and-pool poster. Vosotros and ustedes are an extension: teach them if your class needs them.

The big relief. Only two forms are new: affirmative tú and affirmative vosotros. Everything else is the subjunctive they just learned. Say this first; it lowers the temperature.

tú affirmative = él/ella present. They already know it. Stem changes carry over (cierra, duerme, pide).

vosotros is mainly used in Spain. In Latin America, ustedes covers both informal and formal plural. If your students will mostly speak with Latin Americans, teach vosotros for recognition only.

The negative never comes from the affirmative. *No habla is a statement ("he doesn't speak"), not an order. Expect *no ten and *no di early on and correct them every time.

The eight short tú forms: ven, di, sal, haz, ten, ve, pon, sé. A well-known mnemonic keeps them in that order: "Vin Diesel Has Ten Weapons" (Ven, Di-Sal, Haz, Ten, We-a-Pons → ve, pon, sé).

Homonyms. ve is both ir and ver, and sé is both ser and saber. Context always decides, so it isn't a real problem; say so before someone asks.

Register. A direct imperative isn't rude in Spanish the way it can be in English. Pásame la sal is perfectly friendly at a table.

Teacher notes

Why this slide. Students can't place pronouns around an imperative until they can pick the right pronoun in the first place. Teach this slide in ordinary present-tense sentences, then move to slide 5.

The question test. Ask "¿qué?" to find the direct object and "¿a quién? / ¿para quién?" to find the indirect one. The trap is that a direct object can also be a person with a (Veo a María). The tip on the slide resolves it: if the "what?" is already answered by a thing (las llaves), the person must be the indirect object.

Reflexive isn't a third kind of object so much as an object that happens to be the subject. The left/right table makes this concrete: same verb, but the action lands on someone else or comes back. Many daily-routine verbs work this way (ducharse, levantarse, acostarse), which is exactly the vocabulary of unit 7A's exercise 5.

The summary table is the reassuring part: me, te, nos, os do every job. Only the third person has separate forms, so that's where students need to think.

le → se is purely about sound: Spanish avoids two "l" pronouns in a row. Point out that this se means a él / a ella / a ellos, not "himself". If it's ambiguous, add the person: Se las doy a Pedro.

Leísmo. In parts of Spain you'll hear le for a male person as direct object (Le veo for Lo veo). The RAE accepts it for masculine singular people, but teach lo as the default.

Teacher notes

Book link. Unit 7A states the same two rules with Dúchate, dámelo, póngase and No te duches, no me lo des; those are the rule examples here. The doctor table comes from the unit’s Comunicación box (síntomas / remedios). Use it for a doctor–patient role play, as in activity 8.

The whole slide in one sentence: pronouns go before any conjugated verb in Spanish, and the affirmative imperative is the one exception, where they attach at the end. Framed that way, the negative isn't a new rule; it's just normal Spanish.

Order. Person before thing: me lo, never *lo me. If students know RID (Reflexive, Indirect, Direct), it's the same rule. Slide 4 explains what each pronoun is, so do that slide first if the class is shaky on lo vs le.

le → se. Spanish avoids "le lo" for the sound. Stress that this se is not reflexive: in Léeselo nobody is reading to themselves. Students will then ask who se refers to. If context doesn't make it clear, add a él / a ella / a usted: Dáselo a ella.

The tilde follows ordinary accent rules. Adding syllables pushes the stress back to the third-from-last syllable, and such words always carry a written accent. Dame (2 syllables) needs no accent; dámelo does.

vosotros + os. The -d drops: sentaos. Idos is the only exception. The RAE also accepts iros because it's so widespread in speech, but idos remains the standard written form.

Extension for strong students: nosotros + nos drops the -s: sentemos + nos → sentémonos.

Teacher notes

Book link. In Español en marcha A2 (unit 7C, "¡Que te mejores!") the subjunctive is used only for wishes: ¡Que + subjuntivo! and esperar que, with infinitive for one subject. Sections 1–3 are that core. Everything under Ampliación is B1 preview; skip it, or use it only with fast groups.

¡Que…! These are fixed social formulas, so teach them as chunks first and only then point out the subjunctive inside. Match each to a situation: the illness, the trip, the wedding, the birthday, the meal.

¡Que le aproveche! is what a waiter or a stranger says before you eat. It's usted, which is why it uses le.

The core idea (for the B1 section). The indicative asserts something as fact. The subjunctive presents an idea without asserting it: as something wished for, doubted, judged or still pending. Avoid teaching it as "uncertainty": in Me alegra que estés aquí, your presence is certain, but the sentence is about my reaction to it, not a statement of the fact.

Structure. Two clauses, two different subjects, joined by que. With a single subject Spanish uses the infinitive (Espero llegar), just as English does ("I hope to arrive").

A mnemonic for the triggers: WEIRDO. Wishes, Emotions, Impersonal expressions (es importante que), Recommendations, Doubt/denial, Ojalá. Ojalá comes from Arabic ("if God wills") and always takes the subjunctive.

creer / pensar. Affirmative asserts, so indicative. Negating removes the assertion, so subjunctive.

cuando. For the future, use the subjunctive and never the future tense: *cuando saldré is wrong. But the question ¿Cuándo saldrás? is fine. Students often confuse the two, so contrast them on the board.
